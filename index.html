Week 2 ⏱️ 50 Minutes Total 👥 Group & Peer Exercise

Activity: Research Practice

🎯 Challenge: Designing for Beginner Fitness Customers

📍 Scenario

You are designing a booking and membership website for a local fitness studio catering specifically to beginners.

Your job is not to design the website yet.

Your job is to find out: What might beginner customers need from the service and from the website?

⏱️ 50-Minute Structure

0–5 min

Understand the scenario

Read the scenario. Think about:

  • Who are the users?
  • What might be different about a beginner compared with an experienced gym user?
“Some of your answers are assumptions. Your job is to investigate them.”
5–10 min

What do we need to know?

Each group identify possible research questions.

For example:
  • What concerns do beginners have about joining a fitness studio?
  • What information would help someone decide whether to book a class?
  • What information do beginners want before attending their first session?
  • What might make someone feel more comfortable joining?
  • What types of classes might appeal to beginners?
  • What information do existing fitness studios provide?

A research question tells us what we need to find out.

Research questions

Write 3–5 research questions.

10–25 min

Secondary research

In groups, search for information using reliable online sources.

Find at least two useful sources that help answer one or more of our research questions.

Then complete the table below

For sources, you can use, for instance:
  • NHS
  • Government sources
  • Academic research
  • Professional organisations
  • Fitness industry sources
  • Existing fitness studio websites

Secondary research table

Find useful information from existing sources. Remember: secondary research uses information that has already been collected or published by others.

Research question Source Key finding Why is it useful?
🔍 Check your sources:

Before using information, ask:

  • Who produced the information?
  • Is the source credible?
  • Is it recent enough and relevant to our topic?
  • Does it actually help answer our research question?
25–35 min

Analyse the findings

Now stop searching. Look at what you found and answer:

What does this information tell us about beginner users?

Then:

What might this mean for the service or website?

This is the crucial step. You move from:
Information --> Finding --> Insight --> Possible design implication

From finding to insight

Choose one of your findings.

What did we find? What does this tell us about the users? What might this mean for the service or website?
💡 Example:

Finding: Beginners may be uncertain about what to expect when attending a new fitness class.

Insight: New customers may need reassurance and clear information before booking.

Possible design implication: The website could clearly explain the class, difficulty level, instructor, what happens during the session and what customers should bring.

35–45 min

Primary research

Now, you can conduct primary research with your classmates.

Based on your research, write three questions that you could ask classmates.

For example:
  • Have you ever joined a gym or fitness class?
  • What information would you want to know before attending a class for the first time?
  • What would make you feel more comfortable trying a new fitness class?

Data collection from classmates

Ask your classmates and record some responses.

Question What did people tell us?
45–50 min

Share the insight

Each group gives one finding: “We found that…” and then: “This could mean that…”

For example:
• “We found that beginners can feel uncertain about what to expect from a new fitness class.”
• “This could mean that the website should clearly explain what happens during a first session.”

What did we learn?

“Notice that we didn't start by designing pages. We started by trying to understand the users.”

Week 4 ⏱️ 50 Minutes Total 👥 Group Exercise

Activity – Creating User Personas

🎯 Goal: Translate your Week 2 user research into an evidence-based user persona, moving systematically from user needs to concrete website implications.

📍 Scenario

You are designing a booking and membership website for a local fitness studio catering specifically to beginners.

Last week, you researched potential users and asked your classmates about their experiences, needs and concerns. Today, you will use that research to create a user persona.

📋 Session Steps & Guidance

5 min

Part 1 – Review your research

Look back at your Week 2 research. Identify 3 things you learned about potential users.

💡 Examples:
  • Beginners may be unsure which class is suitable for them.
  • Some people may want to know what happens during their first session.

⚠️ Important: Try to use findings from your research rather than simply guessing what users might want.

5 min

Part 2 – Choose a user type

Discuss your research findings. Could there be different types of beginners?

💡 Examples of Beginner User Types:
  • A university student who wants to become more active.
  • Someone who has never used a fitness studio before.

Choose one type of user to represent in your persona.

20 min

Part 3 – Create your persona

Create a persona based on your research findings.

<
🔍 Worked Example Persona: Meet Sarah

Age: 21

Background: Sarah is a university student who has recently moved to Manchester. She wants to become more active but has never joined a fitness studio before.

Goals: Become more physically active | Find a beginner-friendly activity | Feel comfortable attending her first class

Needs: Clear information about classes | Information about difficulty levels | Information about what to expect

Frustrations / Concerns: Worried that a class might be too difficult | Unsure what to wear or bring | Nervous about attending for the first time

Behaviours: Searches online before choosing a fitness studio | Compares different classes | Looks for information before booking

Website Needs: Clear class descriptions | Beginner-friendly labels | Information about the first visit | Clear booking and pricing information

10 min

Part 4 – Evidence or assumption?

Now check your persona. For each important statement, ask:

"Did our research tell us this, or are we assuming it?"

Mark each statement:

  • R = Supported by research
  • A = Assumption
Example: “Sarah is nervous about attending her first class.”
• If your research showed that beginners have concerns about attending their first class, this could be R.
• If you simply assumed it, it should be A (or rewording to: “Sarah may feel nervous about attending her first class.”).

Why is this important? A persona should be based on evidence about users, not stereotypes or guesses.

10 min

Part 5 – Persona → Website

Look at your persona and identify three important website implications.

Transition Formula:
Week 2: What did we learn about users? --> Week 3: Who are these users? --> Persona: What do they need from our website?

Week 3 ⏱️ 50 Minutes Total 👥 Group Exercise & Analysis

Activity – Competitor Analysis

🎯 Goal: Evaluate existing fitness studio websites through the lens of your users to identify UI/UX strengths, pain points, and strategic design opportunities for your own site.

📍 Scenario

You are continuing the same project:

  • You are designing a booking and membership website for a local fitness studio catering specifically to beginners.
  • Last week, you gathered some information about your potential users.
  • Today, you will look at existing fitness websites and analyse them from your user's perspective.

📋 Session Steps & Guidance

10 min

Part 1 – The 2-minutes challenge

Choose one fitness studio or gym website. Imagine you are the user. You have 2 minutes to find:

⏱️ 60-Second Target Info:
  • What classes are available?
  • How much does it cost?
  • How do you book?
  • Where is it located?
  • Is it suitable for beginners?
🗣️ Discuss in your group:
  • What was easy to find?
  • What was difficult to find?
  • What information was missing?
  • How might this affect your users?
20 min

Part 2 – Analyse two competitors

Choose two relevant fitness websites and compare them from your user's perspective.

⚠️ Think about your users: For each website, ask: “Would our users be able to easily find what they need?”

15 min

Part 3 – From observation to opportunity

Now identify what you can learn from the competitors. Move beyond just listing features to extracting actionable opportunities.

🔍 Worked Example: Observation --> Opportunity

Observation: A competitor clearly explains the difficulty level of each class.

Why does this matter? Our user is a beginner and wants to know whether a class is suitable.

Opportunity: Our website could clearly label classes as Beginner, Beginner–Intermediate, or Intermediate, and clearly explain what each level means.

5 min

Part 4 – Share

Each group shares one useful finding using this structure:

• “We found that…”

• “This could mean that our website should…”

Week 5 ⏱️ 50 Minutes Total 🎨 Visual Storyboarding & UX Opportunity

Week 5 Worklab – Creating a User Storyboard

🎯 Goal: Map your persona's real-world experience, thoughts, and anxieties through a 5–6 scene storyboard to reveal critical moments where your website provides support.

📍 Scenario

You are continuing the project: You are designing a booking and membership website for a local fitness studio catering specifically to beginners.

📋 You already have:
  • Research about potential users
  • A user persona (e.g., Sarah)
  • Competitor analysis

Today: You will create a storyboard showing a user's experience and identifying where your website could help.

📋 Session Steps & Guidance

10 min

Part 1 – Plan your storyboard

Choose a situation involving your persona (for example: looking for a suitable class or preparing for their first visit).

💡 Focus Prompts:
  • Who is the user?
  • What goal are they trying to accomplish?
  • What is currently making it difficult or overwhelming?
25 min

Part 3 – Create your storyboard

Create a storyboard with 5–6 scenes. Drawings should mainly rely on visual depictions rather than walls of text.

✔️ For EACH scene, your visual drawing & notes should reflect:
  1. What is happening? Describe the user's action or situation.
  2. What is the user thinking or feeling? Show the user's perspective & emotion.
  3. What problem or need appears? Identify anything that makes the experience difficult.
  4. Where could the website help? Identify a possible design opportunity.
✅ Your Storyboard SHOULD Include:
  • A clear user and situation
  • A beginning, middle, and end
  • The user's real-world actions
  • The user's thoughts or feelings
  • At least one problem or need
  • Where the website could provide support
❌ AVOID:
  • Drawing ONLY website pages or UI layouts
  • Making the storyboard entirely text (must rely on drawings)
  • Writing only what the user clicks
  • Making the storyboard entirely about the studio business
  • Inventing user behavior without considering your research
🔍 Worked Example Storyboard: Sarah Looking for a Class
Scene 1. What is happening? 2. User Thinking/Feeling 3. Problem / Need 4. Website Support Opportunity
Scene 1 Sarah sits at home feeling sluggish after moving to university. 💭 "I want to exercise, but I don't know where to start." Does not know beginner options in her new city. Search-engine optimization for "beginner fitness".
Scene 2 Searches online on her phone for local studio options. 💭 "Which studio would be suitable for me?" Overwhelmed by hard-core fitness sites. Welcoming homepage hero banner: "Made for Beginners".
Scene 3 Visits studio website and browses class schedules. 💭 "Is this class suitable for someone who is new?" Class names sound complex ("Vinyasa", "HIIT"). Clear difficulty tags (e.g., Level 1 Beginner).
Scene 4 Wants to book but gets nervous about studio etiquette. 💭 "What if I don't know what to do or wear?" Friction: Fear of looking foolish in front of others. Link: "Your First Visit Guide" explaining lockers, clothes & parking.
Scene 5 Reads "Your First Visit" guide and sees photo tour. 💭 "Okay, now I know exactly what to expect!" Uncertainty resolved. Reassurance copy + 1-click "Book Trial Session" CTA.
Scene 6 Selects a beginner class and completes trial booking. 💭 "I feel confident about trying this now." Need for quick confirmation. Instant email receipt + calendar add link.
10 min

Part 4 – From storyboard to design opportunity

Look at your finished scenes and synthesize your visual narrative into a concrete design opportunity statement.

The Core UX Pipeline:
Persona --> User Situation --> Experience --> Problem / Need --> Design Opportunity

Week 7 ⏱️ 50 Minutes Total 🗂️ Information Architecture (IA)

Week 7 Worklab – Card Sorting for Navigation

🎯 Goal: Organize website content and features into intuitive categories based on mental models of beginner users using Open and Closed Card Sorting.

📍 Scenario

You have compiled a list of 15 key content items and features for your beginner fitness studio website. Instead of guessing how to organize the main navigation menu, you will run a Card Sorting Activity with peers to see how users naturally group this information.

📋 50-Minute Card Sorting Activity Structure

0–10 min

Phase 1: Card Deck Setup (15 Content Cards)

Provide each group with 15 index cards (or digital sticky notes) representing key site content:

🎴 The 15 Content Cards:

1. Class Timetable | 2. Beginner Yoga Info | 3. Membership Prices | 4. Instructor Bios | 5. Locker & Shower Info | 6. Parking & Directions | 7. "What to Wear" Guide | 8. Trial Pass Signup | 9. Student Discount Info | 10. Cancellation Policy | 11. Studio Photo Tour | 12. Book a Class | 13. Equipment Provided | 14. Contact Support | 15. Customer Reviews

10–30 min

Phase 2: Open Card Sort Execution

Ask 2 students from another team to act as "Beginner Users." They must group the 15 cards into logical piles and create a Category Header Name for each pile.

30–42 min

Phase 3: Analysis & Disagreements

Identify "Outlier Cards"—cards that users were confused about or tried to place in multiple categories (e.g., Trial Pass Signup).

42–50 min

Phase 4: Draft Main Navigation Architecture

Based on card sorting data, establish 4 top-level navigation menu headers for your site.

Week 8 ⏱️ 50 Minutes Total 🌳 Navigation Validation & Testing

Week 8 Worklab – Tree Testing Your Site Structure

🎯 Goal: Validate whether users can successfully navigate your proposed site hierarchy to complete core tasks, stripping away visual UI design to focus purely on Information Architecture.

📍 Scenario

You have built a proposed navigation tree from last week's card sort. Today, you will run a Tree Test (Reverse Card Sort) with peers to test if users get lost when attempting to complete specific tasks using text-only menu links.

📋 50-Minute Tree Testing Structure

0–10 min

Phase 1: Build the Site Tree & Task Scenarios

Teams write out their text-only navigation tree structure and create 3 specific user tasks.

🌳 Example Site Tree Structure:

• Home

  • First Visit Guide --> What to Wear | Parking & Lockers | Studio Etiquette
  • Classes --> Timetable | Beginner Yoga | Pilates | Class Difficulty Scale
  • Pricing --> Memberships | Trial Pass | Student Offers
10–30 min

Phase 2: Tree Test Execution with Participants

Ask a classmate to complete 3 tasks by clicking/pointing through the text tree (no design elements allowed):

💡 Tutor Demonstration Example (Testing Scenarios):
Task Prompt Expected Target Path Success / Directness Result IA Issue Identified
Task 1: "You are going to your first class tomorrow and want to know if you need to bring your own yoga mat." First Visit Guide --> What to Wear & Bring Success (Direct) Path was intuitive. User immediately clicked "First Visit Guide".
Task 2: "You want to find out if there is a discount for university students." Pricing --> Student Offers Fail / Indirect User clicked "First Visit Guide" first, assuming student discounts were listed under arrival details.
30–42 min

Phase 3: Calculate Success & Directness Rates

Evaluate testing metrics:

  • Success Rate: Did the user reach the correct page?
  • Directness Rate: Did they reach it without backtracking or clicking wrong categories first?
42–50 min

Phase 4: Tree Optimization & Wrap-Up

Re-label or move confusing menu items based on test data before moving to the design in Week 9.
